2. Grammar: mixed conditionals

A mixed conditional joins two different times in one sentence. The if-part and the result do not happen at the same time, so the two verb forms come from different conditionals.

Ask three questions

  1. Is it real or imaginary? Real situations use conditionals 0 and 1. Imaginary ones use 2, 3 or a mixed pattern.
  2. When is the if-part? Is it a past event (if I hadn’t missed the bus) or a present state or fact (if I were taller)?
  3. When is the result? Is it in the past (I would have arrived on time) or is it true now (I wouldn’t be here)?

Try it. I don’t speak Italian. I didn’t understand the waiter yesterday. The if-part is a present ability (if I spoke Italian). The result is in the past (I would have understood). That is mixed B.

Useful variations

Remember

Mixed A (a past cause, a present result): If + had + V3, would + V1. If I had studied law, I would be a lawyer now.

Mixed B (a present state, a past result): If + V2 / were, would have + V3. If I were braver, I would have told her the truth.

3. Typical mistakes

These errors appear again and again in exams and in real speech. Look for the reason behind each correction.

Watch the ’d. It can mean had or would. In If I’d studied, I’d be a doctor the first ’d is had and the second is would. Look at the verb after it: a past participle (studied) means had; a base verb (be) means would.

Same time, no mix. If I had studied, I would have passed is a normal third conditional, because both parts are in the past. A mixed conditional needs a real difference in time between the if-part and the result.

4. Introductory words: all the ways to start a condition

Not every condition starts with if. Choose a group to study it. The small badges show which conditionals each word usually works with: 0, 1, 2, 3, mixed A and mixed B.

Inversion is formal. You drop if and put the auxiliary first: If I had known → Had I known; If I were → Were I; If you should need → Should you need. It is common in writing, speeches and exams.
